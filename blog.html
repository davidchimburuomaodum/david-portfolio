ONLINE SAFETY ARTICLE 001

How to recognise phishing messages before you become a victim

A convincing message can look like it came from your bank, a delivery company, a colleague or a familiar online service. The goal may be to steal your password, money or personal data.

What is phishing?

Phishing is a form of social engineering in which an attacker uses deceptive emails, text messages, websites or social media messages to persuade someone to reveal sensitive information or perform an unsafe action.

Five warning signs to look for

  1. Unexpected urgency. A message claims your account will be closed immediately or demands that you act within minutes.
  2. Suspicious links. A link's displayed text may not match the actual destination. Check the domain carefully before opening it.
  3. Requests for secrets. Treat unexpected requests for passwords, one-time codes, PINs or recovery phrases as serious warning signs.
  4. Unexpected attachments. An unfamiliar document or file may contain harmful content. Do not open it simply because the message looks professional.
  5. Unusual payment requests. Be cautious about requests to transfer money, buy gift cards or change payment details without independent verification.

How to protect yourself

  • Visit an organisation's official website directly instead of following an unexpected message link.
  • Verify unusual requests using a trusted phone number or a separate communication channel.
  • Use unique passwords and enable multifactor authentication where available.
  • Keep your phone, computer, browser and security software updated.
  • Report suspicious messages through the relevant platform or organisation.
Remember: A familiar logo, professional design or correct-looking sender name does not prove that a message is genuine.

If you have already clicked a suspicious link

Stop interacting with the page. If you entered a password, change it through the official website using a trusted device and secure any other accounts that reused it. Contact your bank promptly if you disclosed financial information. If you installed an unfamiliar app or file, stop using it and seek appropriate technical assistance.

Educational information: the safest response depends on the incident and the information exposed.

ACCOUNT PROTECTION ARTICLE 002

Seven practical ways to secure your online accounts

Your email, messaging apps and social accounts can contain valuable personal information. Securing them reduces the risk of impersonation, fraud and account takeover.

1. Use unique passwords

Avoid using the same password across multiple services. A password manager can help you create and maintain long, unique passwords.

2. Enable multifactor authentication

Add a second verification method wherever possible. An authenticator app or security key can provide stronger protection than a password alone. Protect recovery codes as carefully as passwords.

3. Secure your primary email account

Email is often used to reset other passwords. Use a unique password, enable multifactor authentication and review recovery email addresses and phone numbers.

4. Review active sessions

Check account settings for devices and sessions you do not recognise. Sign out unfamiliar sessions and change your password if you suspect unauthorised access.

5. Keep devices updated

Install operating system, browser and application security updates. Updates often fix vulnerabilities that attackers could otherwise exploit.

6. Be careful on public networks

Use trusted networks where possible. Confirm that websites use HTTPS, avoid installing unknown certificates or profiles, and never ignore browser security warnings simply to continue browsing.

7. Prepare for recovery

Keep recovery details current and store backup codes securely. Review important account security settings periodically, before an emergency occurs.

Start today: Secure your primary email account first, because it may control password resets for many other services.
DIGITAL INVESTIGATION ARTICLE 003

Digital forensics explained: why evidence handling matters

When a digital incident occurs, investigators need more than screenshots or assumptions. Reliable findings depend on careful collection, documentation, preservation and analysis of evidence.

What is digital forensics?

Digital forensics is the disciplined process of identifying, collecting, preserving, examining and reporting digital evidence. Depending on the case, evidence may come from computers, phones, storage media, applications, cloud services or system logs.

Why preservation matters

Using a device or changing files can alter data, timestamps, logs or other relevant information. Investigators should follow an appropriate procedure for the incident and avoid unnecessary changes to potential evidence.

Important principles

  • Authority: Obtain proper permission and follow applicable laws and organisational procedures.
  • Documentation: Record who handled the evidence, when it was collected and what actions were performed.
  • Integrity: Use appropriate acquisition methods and integrity checks, such as cryptographic hashes, where suitable.
  • Repeatability: Document methods and tools so that another qualified examiner can understand and assess the process.
  • Confidentiality: Protect personal information and limit access to authorised people.
  • Honest reporting: Distinguish observed facts from interpretations, uncertainty and conclusions.

What should you do after a suspected cyber incident?

If an incident affects a workplace or organisation, notify the responsible security team and follow its incident-response plan. Avoid deleting files, wiping devices or conducting unauthorised investigations. If the situation may involve a crime, seek appropriate professional or law-enforcement guidance.

Key lesson: Evidence is most useful when its origin, integrity, handling and limitations can be explained.

This guide provides general educational information, not legal advice or a substitute for a qualified forensic investigation.
